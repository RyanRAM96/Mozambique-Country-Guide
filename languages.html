Languages

One official language, dozens spoken at home

Portuguese is the language of government, school and the national media, and the one that lets people from Pemba and Maputo understand each other. But for most Mozambicans the language of home is an African one, and many people speak three or more.

First language, share of the population

Where they are spoken

A map in three bands

Mozambique's languages sit in rough bands from north to south, following the history of settlement. The Zambezi and the Save rivers are the two great dividing lines.

North of the Zambezi

The Makhuwa–Lomwe languages dominate, with Swahili influence on the coast.

  • Emakhuwa · Nampula, Niassa, Cabo Delgado, Zambézia
  • Elomwe · Zambézia and southern Nampula
  • Shimakonde · Mueda plateau, Cabo Delgado
  • Ciyao · Niassa, around Lichinga
  • Kimwani · Quirimbas coast
  • Kiswahili · the far northern coast
  • Cinyanja · Lake Niassa shore

The Zambezi and the centre

A meeting zone of Sena, Nyanja and Shona-related languages along the river and the Zimbabwe border.

  • Cisena · lower Zambezi, Sofala, Tete
  • Echuwabo · Quelimane and the coast
  • Cinyungwe · Tete and the middle Zambezi
  • Cinyanja · Angónia and northern Tete
  • Cindau · southern Manica and Sofala
  • Cimanyika, Citewe · Manica highlands

South of the Save

Tsonga languages, with Chopi and Bitonga on the Inhambane coast.

  • Xichangana · Gaza and Maputo
  • Xironga · Maputo city and bay
  • Xitswa · Inhambane and northern Gaza
  • Cicopi · southern Inhambane coast
  • Gitonga · around Inhambane town
A detail in the names

Why so many names start with E-, Ci- or Xi-

Bantu languages sort nouns into classes marked by prefixes. Many language names in Mozambique carry the prefix that means "the language of": Emakhuwa, Elomwe, Cisena, Cinyanja, Xichangana, Shimakonde, Kimwani, Gitonga.

The people themselves take a different prefix. The Makhuwa speak Emakhuwa; the Makonde speak Shimakonde. In English and Portuguese you will often see the bare root, as in "Makhuwa" or "Sena", used for both.

Language and the state

From one language to many

  1. 1975

    Portuguese for unity

    FRELIMO chose Portuguese as the official language, arguing that no single African language could unite a country with so many. Schooling was in Portuguese only.

  2. 1990s

    Pilot schools

    Research showed that many children struggled to learn to read in a language they did not speak at home. A pilot project taught the first school years in local languages in Gaza and Tete.

  3. 2003 onward

    Bilingual education

    Primary schools began teaching early grades in a Mozambican language before moving to Portuguese. The programme now covers more than a dozen languages and has been expanding year by year.

  4. 2004

    National languages in the constitution

    The constitution committed the state to value the national languages as cultural heritage and to promote their use in education and public life.

Other languages you will hear

English is widely taught and used in business and near the borders with English-speaking neighbours. Arabic is learned for religious study in Muslim communities. Gujarati and other South Asian languages are spoken in long-established Indian families, and Mozambican Sign Language is used by the Deaf community.
