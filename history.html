History

Two thousand years in ten eras

Mozambique's story runs from Iron Age farmers and Swahili gold ports, through almost five centuries of Portuguese presence, to a war of liberation, a devastating civil war and the peace and upheavals that followed. Jump to an era below, or read straight through.

Before c. 700 CE

The first peoples

Long before any written record, hunter-gatherers lived across the region. In the first centuries CE they were joined, and largely absorbed, by farming peoples who brought iron and the languages most Mozambicans speak today.

  1. Stone Age

    Hunter-gatherers and rock art

    Stone tools show that people have lived in what is now Mozambique for many thousands of years. Before farming arrived, the land was home to hunter-gatherer peoples related to the San of southern Africa. They left paintings in rock shelters in the western hills, among them the painted shelter at Chinhamapere near the town of Manica.

  2. c. 100–500 CE

    Bantu-speaking farmers arrive

    Farming communities speaking Bantu languages spread into the region from the north and west. They brought iron-working, pottery, cattle and crops such as sorghum and millet. Pottery found at Matola, near Maputo, is among the oldest evidence of these Iron Age farmers in southern Africa. Nearly every African language spoken in Mozambique today descends from theirs.

c. 700–1500

Gold, ivory and the Swahili coast

The coast faced outward to the Indian Ocean. Monsoon winds carried traders from Arabia, Persia and India, and gold from the interior paid for cloth, beads and porcelain.

  1. By c. 800

    Indian Ocean trade begins

    Coastal villages were trading with merchants from the Persian Gulf and beyond, sailing south with the north-east monsoon and home again when the winds reversed. Excavations at Chibuene, near Vilankulo, have found glazed Middle Eastern pottery and glass beads from this early trade.

  2. c. 1200–1600

    Stone towns inland

    Stone-walled centres linked to Great Zimbabwe were built on the routes between the gold fields and the sea. At Manyikeni, in Inhambane province, a stone enclosure housed a local elite who took part in the gold and ivory trade.

  3. 1300s

    Sofala, the gold port

    Gold mined on the Zimbabwe plateau was carried to the coast and shipped north from Sofala, near today's Beira. The trade was dominated by the sultanate of Kilwa, far to the north in present-day Tanzania, which grew rich on it.

  4. 1300s–1400s

    Swahili towns and Islam

    Muslim, Swahili-speaking trading towns grew along the northern and central coast, among them Angoche, Quelimane and a small coral island near the mainland whose ruler would give the whole country its name.

Where the name comes from

Mussa Bin Bique's island

When the Portuguese arrived in 1498, the island off the Nampula coast was ruled by a sheikh usually named in the sources as Mussa Bin Bique (or Musa Mbiki). The Portuguese called the island after him, Moçambique, and over time the name spread to the whole colony and then to the independent nation.

c. 1450–1700

Mutapa and the Portuguese arrival

A powerful Shona state controlled the gold country just as Portuguese ships rounded the Cape. For two centuries Portugal tried to seize the gold, with little success beyond the coast and the Zambezi.

  1. c. 1430–1450

    The Mutapa state

    According to Shona tradition, a ruler named Nyatsimba Mutota moved north from the Great Zimbabwe area and founded a new kingdom. His successors took the title Mwene Mutapa. At its height the state controlled much of the plateau between the Zambezi and the Save, and the trade routes that ran from it to the coast.

  2. 1498

    Vasco da Gama sails past

    On his way to India, Vasco da Gama stopped near Inhambane, which his crew called the Terra da Boa Gente, the land of good people. He then anchored in the Quelimane river and, in March 1498, at Ilha de Moçambique.

  3. 1505

    A fort at Sofala

    Portugal built a fortress at Sofala to take over the gold trade from Swahili merchants. The gold kept flowing along older routes, and the fort never delivered the wealth that was hoped for.

  4. 1507

    Ilha de Moçambique becomes the capital

    The island became Portugal's base in East Africa and a stopping point for ships sailing between Lisbon and Goa. The Fortress of São Sebastião, begun in 1558, still stands. The island's stone and coral-rag towns have been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1991.

  5. 1530s

    Sena and Tete

    Portuguese trading posts were founded at Sena and Tete on the Zambezi, the river road into the gold and ivory country.

  6. 1569–1573

    Barreto's failed conquest

    Francisco Barreto led a Portuguese army up the Zambezi to seize the Mutapa gold mines. Fever, heat and armed resistance destroyed the expedition.

  7. 1607–1608

    The Dutch sieges

    Dutch fleets besieged Ilha de Moçambique twice, hoping to take over Portugal's route to India. The fortress held both times.

  8. 1629

    Mutapa becomes a vassal

    Portugal backed Mavura, a claimant to the Mutapa throne, who signed a treaty accepting Portuguese overlordship. The Mutapa state declined through the rest of the century.

  9. 1693–1695

    Changamire drives Portugal out

    Changamire Dombo, founder of the Rozvi state, drove the Portuguese from their trading fairs on the plateau. Portuguese influence was confined largely to the Zambezi valley and the coast for the next two centuries.

c. 1600–1880s

Prazos, ivory and the slave trade

Portuguese control was thin and often nominal. Power lay with estate lords on the Zambezi, African states, and the traders who carried ivory and, increasingly, captives to the coast.

  1. 1600s–1800s

    The prazos of the Zambezi

    Portugal granted large estates in the Zambezi valley, the prazos da Coroa, on leases that officially passed down through the female line. Many prazo holders were Afro-Portuguese or Goan. They ruled like African chiefs, collected tribute, and kept private armies of enslaved soldiers known as the chikunda.

  2. 1752

    A separate colony

    Mozambique was separated from the Portuguese government in Goa, India, and given its own governor.

  3. 1700s–1800s

    The slave trade

    Demand for enslaved labour grew on the French sugar islands of Mauritius and Réunion, in Brazil and in Cuba. Ilha de Moçambique, Quelimane, Ibo and Inhambane became slaving ports, and Yao and Makhuwa traders brought captives from the interior. Portugal banned the export of slaves in 1836, but the trade continued illegally for decades and later in the disguise of "contract labour".

  4. 1820s–1830s

    The Gaza Empire

    Wars and migrations among Nguni-speaking peoples to the south, known as the Mfecane, sent armed groups north. One leader, Soshangane, conquered much of southern Mozambique and founded the Gaza Empire, which dominated the land between the Limpopo and the Zambezi for most of the century. The Shangaan (Changana) people take their name from him.

  5. 1850s–1888

    The Zambezi wars

    Afro-Portuguese warlord families, such as the da Cruz of Massangano, fought the Portuguese state and defeated several expeditions sent against them before their stronghold fell in 1888.

1885–1930

Conquest and the chartered companies

In the Scramble for Africa, Portugal had to occupy what it claimed. It fought its way inland, fixed the borders with Britain, and handed much of the country to private companies.

  1. 1884–1885

    The Berlin Conference

    European powers agreed that colonial claims needed "effective occupation". Portugal's centuries-old presence on the coast was no longer enough, and it began a long and costly conquest of the interior.

  2. 1890–1891

    The British Ultimatum and the borders

    Portugal hoped to link Mozambique and Angola across the continent, a plan drawn on its "Pink Map". A British ultimatum in January 1890 ended that ambition, and an Anglo-Portuguese treaty in 1891 fixed most of Mozambique's present borders.

  3. 1891

    Chartered companies

    Portugal handed huge territories to private companies. The Mozambique Company ran Manica and Sofala until 1942, and the Niassa Company ran the far north until 1929. They collected taxes and supplied forced labour, often investing little in return.

  4. 1895

    The fall of Gaza

    Portuguese forces defeated the Gaza armies at Marracuene, Magul and Coolela. On 28 December 1895 Mouzinho de Albuquerque captured the emperor Ngungunhane at Chaimite. He was exiled to the Azores and died there in 1906. In Mozambique he is remembered as a hero of resistance.

  5. 1898

    The capital moves south

    The capital moved from Ilha de Moçambique to Lourenço Marques, today's Maputo, a port growing fast on trade with the gold mines of the Transvaal.

  6. 1909

    Men for the mines

    A convention between Portugal and the Transvaal linked traffic through the port of Lourenço Marques to the recruitment of Mozambican men for South Africa's gold mines. Migrant mine labour shaped family life in the south for most of the century.

  7. 1917–1918

    The Barue rising and the First World War

    In central Mozambique, the Barue rebellion against forced labour and wartime conscription spread across the Zambezi valley before it was crushed. In the same years, German troops from East Africa marched deep into northern Mozambique.

1930–1960

The Estado Novo colony

Under António de Oliveira Salazar's dictatorship, Mozambique was run to serve Portugal's economy. Most Africans lived under separate laws, paid a head tax and could be forced to work.

  1. 1930

    The Colonial Act

    The Colonial Act centralised control in Lisbon and tied the colonies' economies to the needs of Portugal, a system that the Estado Novo regime kept for four decades.

  2. Until 1961

    The indigenato

    Africans were classed as indígenas, subject to separate laws, pass controls and chibalo, forced labour. A few could apply to become assimilados by proving they spoke Portuguese and lived a "civilised" way of life. By the 1950s only a tiny fraction of the population had that status.

  3. 1938

    Forced cotton

    Peasant families in the north and centre were made to grow cotton and sell it at low fixed prices to concession companies. It left less land and time for food crops, caused hunger, and bred deep resentment.

  4. 1951

    An "overseas province"

    Portugal renamed its colonies overseas provinces to deflect pressure to decolonise. White settlement grew fast after the Second World War, reaching roughly 200,000 people by the early 1970s.

  5. 1940s–1950s

    New voices

    Dockworkers in Lourenço Marques went on strike in 1947 and 1956. In the city's suburbs, the poet José Craveirinha and the painter Malangatana Ngwenya began to give form to a Mozambican identity. Both were later jailed by the secret police.

1960–1975

The war for independence

As Britain and France withdrew from Africa, Portugal refused. A united liberation front took up arms in the north and fought for ten years, until the dictatorship in Lisbon collapsed.

  1. 16 Jun 1960

    The Mueda massacre

    At Mueda, on the Makonde plateau, Portuguese troops opened fire on a crowd that had gathered to press demands on the district administrator. Estimates of the dead range from dozens to several hundred. For many, the killings proved that peaceful protest would not work.

  2. 25 Jun 1962

    FRELIMO is founded

    Three exile movements merged in Dar es Salaam, Tanganyika, to form the Mozambique Liberation Front, FRELIMO. Its first president was Eduardo Mondlane, a sociologist who had studied in South Africa, Portugal and the United States and worked for the United Nations.

  3. 25 Sep 1964

    The armed struggle begins

    FRELIMO guerrillas attacked the Portuguese administrative post at Chai in Cabo Delgado. The war spread through Cabo Delgado and Niassa, where FRELIMO ran "liberated zones" with their own schools, clinics and farms.

  4. 1968

    A new front and a congress

    FRELIMO opened a front in Tete province and held its second congress inside Mozambique, in Niassa.

  5. 3 Feb 1969

    Mondlane assassinated

    Eduardo Mondlane was killed in Dar es Salaam by a bomb hidden in a book mailed to him. The anniversary is now Heroes' Day.

  6. 1970

    Machel takes the lead

    Samora Machel, the movement's military commander, became president of FRELIMO. Portugal launched Operation Gordian Knot, its largest offensive of the war, against FRELIMO's northern bases. It failed to end the insurgency.

  7. 7 Apr 1971

    Josina Machel dies

    Josina Machel, a leader of FRELIMO's women's detachment and Samora Machel's wife, died of illness at 25. The date is celebrated as Mozambican Women's Day.

  8. 16 Dec 1972

    The Wiriyamu massacre

    Portuguese troops killed around 400 villagers at Wiriyamu in Tete province. When the massacre was reported in The Times of London in 1973, it caused an international outcry.

  9. 25 Apr 1974

    The Carnation Revolution

    Junior officers, worn down by colonial wars in Mozambique, Angola and Guinea-Bissau, overthrew the dictatorship in Lisbon.

  10. 7 Sep 1974

    The Lusaka Accord

    Portugal and FRELIMO agreed on a transfer of power without elections. A transitional government led by Joaquim Chissano took office.

  11. 25 Jun 1975

    Independence

    The People's Republic of Mozambique was proclaimed in Lourenço Marques, with Samora Machel as its first president.

1975–1992

Independence and civil war

The new state set out to transform society, but it faced a collapsing economy, hostile white-ruled neighbours and a rebel movement they armed. The war that followed devastated the countryside.

  1. 1975–1976

    Exodus and nationalisation

    Most Portuguese settlers left within a year, taking skills and capital with them. The government nationalised land, schools, health care and rented housing, and launched mass literacy and vaccination campaigns.

  2. 1976

    Sanctions against Rhodesia

    Mozambique closed its border with white-ruled Rhodesia, applying UN sanctions, and gave bases to Zimbabwean guerrillas. Rhodesia replied with raids across the border. Lourenço Marques was renamed Maputo the same year.

  3. 1977

    A Marxist-Leninist party

    FRELIMO's third congress turned the movement into a Marxist-Leninist vanguard party. Rural families were encouraged, and at times forced, into communal villages and state farms.

  4. 1977–1992

    The civil war

    Rhodesian intelligence organised an armed opposition, the Mozambican National Resistance (RENAMO). After Zimbabwe's independence in 1980, apartheid South Africa became its main backer. The war reached every province. RENAMO attacked roads, railways, schools and clinics, and both sides abused civilians. Together with drought and famine, the war cost about a million lives, and some five million people were driven from their homes.

  5. 16 Mar 1984

    The Nkomati Accord

    Mozambique and South Africa signed a non-aggression pact. South Africa promised to stop supporting RENAMO but secretly kept supplying it. The same year Mozambique joined the IMF and the World Bank.

  6. 19 Oct 1986

    Samora Machel is killed

    President Machel died when his plane crashed at Mbuzini, just inside South Africa, on the way home from a summit in Zambia. The cause of the crash has never been settled. Joaquim Chissano succeeded him.

  7. 1987–1990

    Reform and a new constitution

    An economic recovery programme began the move away from central planning. The 1990 constitution introduced multi-party politics, a market economy and a free press, and the country became the Republic of Mozambique.

  8. 4 Oct 1992

    The Rome General Peace Accords

    After two years of talks mediated by the Community of Sant'Egidio, the Italian government and the Catholic Church, President Chissano and RENAMO leader Afonso Dhlakama signed a peace agreement in Rome.

1992–2016

Peace, elections and growth

Mozambique became one of Africa's most-cited post-war success stories, with regular elections and fast economic growth. Floods, inequality and corruption tested that reputation.

  1. 1992–1994

    UN peacekeeping

    The UN mission ONUMOZ oversaw the ceasefire, the demobilisation of both armies and the return of well over a million refugees from neighbouring countries.

  2. Oct 1994

    The first multi-party elections

    Chissano and FRELIMO won. RENAMO accepted the result and became the main opposition party in parliament.

  3. 1995–1996

    New memberships

    Mozambique joined the Commonwealth in 1995, the first member never ruled by Britain. A year later it was a founding member of the Community of Portuguese Language Countries.

  4. Feb–Mar 2000

    The great floods

    Weeks of heavy rain and Cyclone Eline flooded the Limpopo and Save valleys. Hundreds of people died and hundreds of thousands lost their homes. The image of Rosita Mabuiango, born in a tree above the floodwater and lifted to safety by helicopter, was seen around the world.

  5. 2005

    Guebuza succeeds Chissano

    Armando Guebuza became president after Chissano stepped down. In 2007 Chissano received the first Ibrahim Prize for Achievement in African Leadership.

  6. 2007

    Cahora Bassa comes home

    Portugal handed majority control of the Cahora Bassa hydroelectric company to Mozambique, more than thirty years after independence.

  7. 2010–2013

    Gas under the sea

    Exploration in the Rovuma Basin off Cabo Delgado found some of the largest natural gas fields discovered anywhere in years, raising hopes of rapid wealth.

  8. 2013–2016

    Renewed fighting

    RENAMO fighters clashed with government forces in the centre of the country over disputed elections and the sharing of power, until a truce at the end of 2016.

  9. 2016

    The hidden debts

    It emerged that state-linked companies had secretly borrowed about US$2 billion in 2013–2014, backed by government guarantees kept from parliament and donors. Aid was suspended and the economy slumped. Former finance minister Manuel Chang was convicted in a US court in 2024 for his part in the scheme.

2017–today

Recent years

An insurgency in the far north, a run of destructive cyclones, a second peace deal with RENAMO and a bitterly disputed election have defined the last decade.

  1. Oct 2017

    Insurgency in Cabo Delgado

    Armed Islamist militants attacked police posts in Mocímboa da Praia. The insurgency spread across the province, and the group later declared allegiance to the Islamic State. Thousands of people have been killed and, at its peak, around a million were forced from their homes.

  2. May 2018

    Dhlakama dies

    RENAMO's long-time leader Afonso Dhlakama died in his mountain base in Gorongosa. Ossufo Momade succeeded him.

  3. Mar–Apr 2019

    Cyclones Idai and Kenneth

    Cyclone Idai struck Beira on 14–15 March, flooding much of central Mozambique and killing more than 600 people in the country. Six weeks later Cyclone Kenneth hit Cabo Delgado. It was the first time two strong cyclones had struck Mozambique in a single season.

  4. 6 Aug 2019

    The Maputo peace accord

    President Filipe Nyusi and Ossufo Momade signed a new peace agreement. The disarmament of RENAMO's last fighters was completed in 2023.

  5. 2021

    Palma and foreign troops

    Insurgents attacked the town of Palma, beside the main onshore gas project, which was suspended. From July, troops from Rwanda and from the Southern African Development Community deployed and retook key towns.

  6. 2022–2023

    Gas exports, a Security Council seat, Cyclone Freddy

    The first cargo of liquefied natural gas left a floating plant off Cabo Delgado in late 2022. In 2023 Mozambique took up a seat on the UN Security Council for the first time, and Cyclone Freddy, the longest-lasting tropical cyclone on record, struck the country twice.

  7. Oct 2024

    A disputed election

    FRELIMO's candidate Daniel Chapo was declared winner of the presidential election. The independent candidate Venâncio Mondlane rejected the result, and two of his aides were shot dead in Maputo. Months of protests followed, in which hundreds of people were killed, most of them by the security forces. Chapo was sworn in on 15 January 2025.

  8. 25 Jun 2025

    Fifty years of independence

    Mozambique marked half a century as an independent nation.

Key figures

People who shaped the story

c. 1850–1906

Ngungunhane

Last emperor of Gaza. Captured by the Portuguese at Chaimite in 1895 and exiled to the Azores. In 1985 an urn said to hold his remains was brought back from the Azores to Maputo.

1920–1969

Eduardo Mondlane

Founding president of FRELIMO. A scholar who gave up an academic career in the United States to lead the independence movement. Maputo's main university bears his name.

1933–1986

Samora Machel

Guerrilla commander and first president. A former nurse, known for his long, fiery speeches and his drive for literacy and health care. Killed in a plane crash in 1986.

1945–1971

Josina Machel

Leader of FRELIMO's women's detachment, who argued that liberation must include the liberation of women. Mozambican Women's Day, 7 April, honours her.

Born 1939

Joaquim Chissano

President from 1986 to 2005. Negotiated the 1992 peace accords, oversaw the first multi-party elections, and later mediated conflicts across Africa.

Born 1945

Graça Machel

Mozambique's first minister of education, who helped expand schooling after independence. Widow of Samora Machel and later wife of Nelson Mandela; a global advocate for children's rights.

1953–2018

Afonso Dhlakama

Leader of RENAMO from 1979 until his death. Signed the 1992 peace accords and ran for president in five elections.

1936–2011

Malangatana Ngwenya

Painter and poet whose crowded, visionary canvases made him Mozambique's best-known artist. Jailed by the colonial secret police in the 1960s.
