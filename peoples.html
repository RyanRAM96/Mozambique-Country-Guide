Peoples & Culture

Many peoples, one nation

Almost all Mozambicans belong to Bantu-speaking African peoples, each with its own language, history and customs. Borders drawn in the 1890s cut across many of them, so the same peoples often live in Malawi, Tanzania, Zimbabwe or South Africa too.

Peoples of Mozambique

NorthEmakhuwa

Makhuwa

The largest people in Mozambique, spread across Nampula, Niassa, Cabo Delgado and Zambézia. Traditionally matrilineal, with land and lineage passing through the mother's family. On the coast many are Muslim, after centuries of Swahili contact. Makhuwa women, especially on Ilha de Moçambique, are known for mussiro, a white paste of ground wood worn on the face to protect and beautify the skin.

NorthShimakonde

Makonde

People of the Mueda plateau in Cabo Delgado, also found in Tanzania. Famous for sculpture carved from African blackwood and for mapiko, a masked dance in which a dancer in a carved helmet mask embodies a spirit. The liberation war began on their plateau, and many Makonde fought in it.

NorthCiyao

Yao

People of the Niassa highlands around Lichinga, also living in Malawi and Tanzania. In the 1800s Yao chiefs ran caravans of ivory, and of captives, from Lake Niassa to the coast, and most Yao became Muslim in that period.

NorthKimwani

Mwani

A coastal people of the Quirimbas shore in Cabo Delgado, living by fishing and trade. Their Swahili-related language takes its name from the word for the coast. In the far north, around Palma, some people speak Kiswahili itself.

North & CentreCinyanja

Nyanja and Chewa

Living along Lake Niassa and in the Angónia highlands of Tete, closely related to the Chewa of Malawi and Zambia. Their Nyau societies perform the Gule Wamkulu masked dances, recognised by UNESCO as intangible heritage shared by the three countries.

CentreElomwe

Lomwe

Close relatives of the Makhuwa, living mainly in Zambézia and southern Nampula. Mount Namuli is honoured as the place where the Lomwe and Makhuwa peoples began. Many Lomwe also live in southern Malawi, where they moved around 1900.

CentreCisena

Sena

The main people of the lower Zambezi valley in Sofala, Tete and Zambézia. Their history is bound up with the river trade, the Portuguese town of Sena and the prazo estates.

CentreEchuwabo

Chuwabo

The people of Quelimane and its hinterland, a meeting point of Swahili, Portuguese and Zambezi cultures for centuries.

CentreCinyungwe

Nyungwe

People of the middle Zambezi around the city of Tete, near the old Portuguese trading post and the Cahora Bassa gorge.

CentreCindau

Ndau

A Shona-speaking people of southern Manica and Sofala, also living across the border in Zimbabwe. Their country was the seat of the Gaza Empire's rulers until Ngungunhane moved his capital south in 1889.

CentreShona dialects

Manyika and Teve

Shona peoples of the Manica highlands, once part of the Mutapa world. The gold of Manica drew Swahili traders and then the Portuguese for centuries.

SouthXichangana

Changana

The largest people of the south, in Gaza and Maputo, named after Soshangane, founder of the Gaza Empire. Generations of Changana men worked in South Africa's gold mines, and many families have relatives across the border.

SouthXironga

Ronga

The first inhabitants of the Maputo area. Ronga songs and rhythms shaped marrabenta, the dance music born in the capital's suburbs.

SouthXitswa

Tswa

A Tsonga people of the Inhambane interior and northern Gaza, related to the Changana and Ronga.

SouthCicopi

Chopi

People of the southern Inhambane coast and makers of the timbila, wooden xylophones with gourd resonators, played in large orchestras with dancers. UNESCO recognised Chopi timbila as a masterpiece of the world's intangible heritage in 2005.

SouthGitonga

Bitonga

A small people living around Inhambane Bay, one of the oldest trading harbours on the coast.

Other communities

A meeting place of oceans and empires

Small but long-established communities add to the mix. Mixed-race Mozambicans (mestiços) and people of Portuguese descent live mostly in the cities. Indian families, both Hindu and Muslim and many from Gujarat, have traded on the coast for centuries, alongside Goans who came under Portuguese rule. Chinese communities in Beira and Maputo date back to the late 1800s.

Together these groups make up well under 1% of the population, but they have had a large influence on trade, food and city life.

Religion

Faith

Mozambique is a secular state. Christianity is strongest in the south and centre and Islam in the north and along the coast, and many people combine either with respect for ancestors and traditional healers.

Culture

Music, art, food and famous names

Music

Timbila

Chopi xylophone orchestras of up to thirty instruments, playing long suites called ngodo with dancers and singers. The master composer Venâncio Mbande kept the tradition alive and took it to audiences abroad.

Music

Marrabenta

Fast, guitar-driven dance music that grew up in the suburbs of Lourenço Marques from the 1930s, mixing Ronga rhythms with Portuguese and South African sounds. Fany Mpfumo, Wazimbo and the band Ghorwane are among its best-known names.

Art

Makonde sculpture

Carvings in dense black African blackwood, from realistic figures to the towering "tree of life" pieces in which dozens of interlocking people climb over one another.

Dress

Capulana

A length of printed cotton worn as a wrap skirt, used to carry babies, and given as a gift. Each print has a name and a meaning, and the cloth also makes shirts and dresses for special occasions.

Food

At the table

Xima, a stiff maize porridge, is the everyday staple. Matapa is cassava leaves pounded and cooked with peanuts and coconut milk, often with crab or prawns. Piri-piri, the fiery small chilli, seasons grilled chicken and the country's famous prawns, and frango à zambeziana is chicken in coconut sauce from Quelimane.

Literature

Writers

The poet José Craveirinha became the first African to win the Camões Prize, the top award in Portuguese literature, in 1991. Mia Couto, author of Sleepwalking Land, won it in 2013, and Paulina Chiziane, the first Mozambican woman to publish a novel, in 2021.

Art & architecture

Painters and builders

The painter Malangatana Ngwenya and the sculptor Alberto Chissano led modern Mozambican art. In Maputo, the playful concrete buildings of the architect Pancho Guedes still stand out on the city's avenues.

Sport

Champions

Maria de Lurdes Mutola won the 800 m at the Sydney Olympics in 2000, the country's first Olympic gold. Eusébio, born in Lourenço Marques in 1942, became one of the greatest footballers of all time with Benfica and Portugal.
