Geography

A long country facing the sea

Mozambique stretches nearly 2,000 km from the Rovuma river in the north to the South African border, between the high plateaus of the interior and the warm Mozambique Channel. Rivers from half a dozen countries cross it to reach the Indian Ocean.

Landforms

From coastal plain to mountain

The land rises in steps from the sea towards the western borders. The coastal plain is widest in the south, where it covers almost everything below the Save river; in the north, plateaus come much closer to the coast.

Below 200 m

Coastal lowlands

A broad plain of sandy soils, lagoons, swamps and river floodplains that covers more than 40% of the country. It holds most of the large cities and the fertile lower valleys of the Limpopo, Save and Zambezi.

200–1,000 m

Plateaus

Rolling uplands dotted with granite inselbergs, the bare domes of rock that rise from the bush across Nampula and Niassa. Much of this country is covered in miombo woodland.

Above 1,000 m

Highlands

The Chimanimani range and Manica highlands on the Zimbabwe border, the Angónia plateau in Tete, the Lichinga plateau in Niassa, and the Namuli massif in Zambézia, which reaches 2,419 m.

The rift

End of the Great Rift Valley

The East African Rift reaches its southern end here. Lake Niassa fills one arm of it, and the Urema trough in Gorongosa National Park is another.

Rivers & lakes

Where Africa's rivers meet the sea

Around two dozen major rivers cross Mozambique, most of them rising in neighbouring countries. That brings water and fertile silt, but also floods when heavy rain falls upstream.

Lake

Lake Niassa

Known as Lake Malawi in Malawi, it is one of the deepest lakes in Africa. Mozambique holds part of the eastern shore, a quiet stretch of beaches and fishing villages that has been a protected reserve since 2011. The lake holds hundreds of kinds of brightly coloured cichlid fish found nowhere else.

Reservoir

Cahora Bassa

Built by the Portuguese between 1969 and 1974 in a gorge of the Zambezi in Tete, the dam holds back a lake nearly 300 km long. It is one of Africa's largest hydroelectric schemes, and much of its power is sold to South Africa.

Climate

Two seasons and a cyclone belt

The climate is tropical in the north and centre and subtropical in the far south. Rain falls mostly in the hot season, and it is heavier in the north and in the highlands. The interior of Gaza and parts of Tete are the driest regions.

Cyclone season · November to April, peaking January to March

Tropical cyclones form over the warm waters of the south-west Indian Ocean and the Mozambique Channel. The long, low coast and the wide river deltas make Mozambique one of the countries most exposed to them. Idai (2019) flooded Beira and the Púnguè and Búzi valleys; Kenneth (2019) struck Cabo Delgado; Freddy (2023) made landfall twice.

Droughts are also common, especially in the south, and scientists expect climate change to make both extremes more severe. Mangrove restoration and early warning systems are part of the national response.

Coast & islands

About 2,470 km of coastline

The north has coral islands and deep bays, the centre has muddy deltas and mangroves, and the south has high dunes, lagoons and long sandy beaches.

Cabo Delgado

Quirimbas Archipelago

Around thirty coral islands strung along the northern coast. Ibo island has a star-shaped fort and a faded colonial town that was once a busy trading port.

Nampula

Ilha de Moçambique

A 3 km island joined to the mainland by a long bridge. Its northern half is the old stone town; the southern half, Makuti town, is built of reed and palm thatch. A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1991.

Inhambane

Bazaruto Archipelago

Islands of towering dunes, freshwater lakes and seagrass meadows off Vilankulo, protected as a national park since 1971.

Maputo

Inhaca Island

At the mouth of Maputo Bay, with coral reefs, mangroves and a marine biology station run by Eduardo Mondlane University.

Natural resources

Gas, coal, rubies and farmland

Most Mozambicans make a living from farming, largely on small family plots of maize, cassava, beans and groundnuts. The country's mineral and energy wealth has grown fast since 2000, though it has created relatively few jobs.
